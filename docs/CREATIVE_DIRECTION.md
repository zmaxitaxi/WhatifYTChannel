# AETHER creative direction

Accepted direction from the October 31, 2026 draft reviews. Use this guide for
storyboards, models, cameras, captions, sound, and export review. The storyboard
planner receives this file as project guidance. Numerical layout and timing
limits remain enforced by the production contract.

## What we are making

University what-ifs told through imaginative, understandable, moving 3D worlds.
One changed decision should produce a chain of visible consequences: a different
city, technology, routine, opportunity, or problem. The viewer should understand
the premise immediately and keep discovering why the next consequence matters.

The Stanford-in-Boston draft establishes a visual and technical baseline. Its
caption writing is a first draft, not the finished editorial standard. Future
captions need stronger curiosity, clearer causal links, and more satisfying
payoffs. These are creative requirements, not measured retention guarantees.

## Story and pacing

- Open directly on a concise “What if…?” question and a relevant action. Make
  the changed rule or decision understandable in the first few seconds.
- Develop a complete alternate timeline or consequence chain. Show what changes,
  why it changes, and who experiences the result. A new setting needs more than
  different weather or a familiar campus placed on another empty plane.
- Use surprising but connected developments. A Boston tech boom can change a
  workshop, investment destination, commute, and apartment hunt. Establish the
  assumptions connecting these events; geography alone explains very little.
- Include a human point of view: a student arriving, an engineer working, a
  founder shipping something, or a graduate looking for a room.
- Vary settings, scale, viewpoint, and action as the story advances. Wide campus
  shots, close props, working interiors, streets, and transport should reveal
  different information. Repeated views need a changed state or a new discovery.
- Pay off the opening question within the episode. End on an earned twist,
  tradeoff, or specific next question. Leave the viewer curious after receiving
  an answer; do not withhold the answer behind “watch part two.”
- Keep historical anchors, fictional causal assumptions, and invented outcomes
  distinct in the fact pack and review workspace. Do not invent statistics,
  rankings, historical events, company origins, or forecasts as facts.

## Captions that make the next beat matter

Captions carry the story during muted viewing. Readability is necessary;
interesting writing also requires the following:

- Give each caption a job: establish the change, explain a cause, reveal an
  outcome, complicate it, or deliver a payoff. Replace labels and generic
  summaries with concrete people, objects, actions, and consequences.
- Connect beats. A question should receive an answer; a reveal should change
  what the viewer expects next. Build several small payoffs across the episode
  instead of saving all useful information for the last frame.
- Make curiosity specific. “Who gets the factories?” creates a clear promise;
  “Everything changes” does not. Avoid unsupported superlatives and “you won't
  believe what happens next.”
- Vary sentence rhythm. Mix a concise setup, a short reveal, a causal statement,
  and a human consequence. Do not turn every caption into a question or repeat
  the same setup/reveal formula on every cut.
- Let the image establish visible details while the caption adds significance.
  A walking student needs a reason for the journey, not the label “a student.”
- Break lines at meaningful phrase boundaries. Preserve negations, names, and
  qualifiers. Do not split a thought merely to make line lengths symmetrical.
- Use no more than two readable lines and at most 2.5 words per second. Shorten
  the wording or give it more time rather than shrinking or accelerating text.
- Use bold, clear sans-serif text on a high-contrast plate, with restrained
  emphasis on selected words. The active baseline is DM Sans at 72 px, with a
  60 px minimum when fitting. Check actual phone playback and essential margins.
- Keep the caption stable long enough to read. Word-by-word flashing, constant
  bouncing, excessive capitalization, and competing headings are not the style.
- Review the caption sequence by itself for narrative momentum, then review it
  muted with the animation for comprehension and payoff.

Untimed copy examples for a future rewrite, within an explicitly fictional
Stanford timeline:

| Flat or vague line | More concrete direction |
|---|---|
| “Labs spark startups. Startups fund labs.” | “Graduates stay. Their workshops become companies.” Then show why investors follow. |
| “Boston ships ideas. The world plugs in.” | “Built in Boston. Shipped around the world.” Match the cargo to the invention already introduced. |
| “One campus. A different tech world.” | “Boston gets the boom. Now try finding a room.” Pay off the success through a student's problem. |
| “Which coast would you choose?” | Ask about the demonstrated tradeoff: who gets to stay in the city they helped build? |

Examples need timing, evidence/fiction review, and visual support before use;
they are not replacement captions for the already rendered MP4.

## Models, framing, and motion

- Prefer the project's recognizable stylized models. Use clear faces, strong
  silhouettes, coherent materials, and enough detail to explain an object's role.
  Extreme surface realism is unnecessary.
- Keep human, vehicle, and building scale believable. Make the relevant campus
  or landmark dominate the composition, with the person and action readable in
  front of it. Populate the surroundings when they contribute context.
- Avoid empty backdrops. Give outdoor scenes intentional terrain, buildings,
  paths, people, vegetation, clouds, sun, and depth where appropriate. Reserve
  clear space for the year display and captions.
- Give every scene purposeful visible action throughout its duration: translating
  walkers, working hands, turning machinery, construction, loading, or transport.
  A slow camera drift or tiny decorative oscillation is insufficient by itself.
- Walking must travel through space with coordinated limbs and a gait related
  to distance. Vary paths and phases; avoid synchronized copies and walking in
  place. Check people against furniture, other people, and the camera frame.
- Boats and vehicles need clear routes, sensible heading, and wheel/wake motion.
  Check routes against quays, piers, ground, and caption plates.
- Continue camera travel through cuts. Preserve scene action across adjacent
  detail cuts instead of restarting it. Avoid long static holds or eased tails
  that read as stand-stills.
- Sample from absolute frame time and scene progress. Backward seeking must
  reconstruct the same state and pixels. Freeze models and the production bundle
  while capturing; coordinate model work with the permanent Astra High chat.

## Sound and overlays

- Give different scenes distinct, relevant sound identities through brief,
  restrained effects. Synchronize important contacts, arrivals, and reveals.
- Leave quiet gaps. Use soft attacks, faded tails, modest stereo width, and
  comfortable levels. Avoid constant birds, repeated chirps, continuous wind,
  crowd beds, loud whooshes, and a sound on every caption change.
- The active cut uses one 0.8–1.4-second effect per distinct scene, totaling
  13.7 seconds across 72 seconds. Treat that as a reference for restraint, not
  a mandatory cue count for every story.
- Preserve the active −22 LUFS target and encoded true peak at or below −1 dBTP.
  Inspect the encoded AAC, not just the WAV. Assets need source/rights/hash records.
- Use a clear year/location display for historical progression. Other stories
  may need a meaningful elapsed-time or countdown display. Remove redundant
  “Imagined Scenario” headers and decorative title cards.
- Keep chapter footers, production disclaimers, AETHER watermarks, and progress
  lines out of the exported video. Keep research and production details in the
  review workspace. The opening premise and story must make the fiction clear.

## Delivery and review

- Deliver one 1080 × 1920, constant 30 fps, 9:16 MP4, normally 45–75 seconds.
  Preserve the established H.264/AAC export and obvious website Export MP4 control.
- Use the same resolution and frame rate for short diagnostic segments. Keep
  generated videos, job databases, dependencies, and temporary media out of Git.
- Review motion sequences and the full encoded video. Exact frame uniqueness
  alone cannot establish that an action is visible or an edit is engaging.
- Inspect text safety, clipping, phone-size recognition, action continuity,
  repeated frames, and frozen stretches. The latest cut passed a full-video
  check for freezes of at least 0.5 seconds at a −50 dB threshold; retain visual
  judgment alongside that measurement.
- Verify all delivery checks and an actual website download with a matching
  checksum. Approval and publishing stay human-controlled.
- A new topic is a creative proposal until its research and supported scene
  capabilities are established. This guide does not authorize invented data,
  unsupported renderer operations, or changes to fixed contract fields.
